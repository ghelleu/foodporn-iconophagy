From Foodporn to Iconophagy

When Memory Becomes Tradable, and Experience Turns Into Asset

Guillaume Helleu
Design Researcher, HEAD–Genève
June 2026
Artist-decorated macaron — ephemeral artwork designed for consumption

Luxury goods are built to last. A Hermès bag, a Patek Philippe watch, a Basquiat painting — their value lies in permanence, in their ability to survive their owner, to be collected, authenticated, passed down through generations.

Food disappears the moment you eat it.

Yet haute cuisine commands luxury prices. A three-Michelin-star tasting menu costs as much as a limited-edition sneaker, but you can't resell it. You can't authenticate it. You can't even prove you ate it — except through an image.

This tension defines contemporary gastronomy: how do you preserve, share, and capitalize on an experience designed to vanish?

The Ephemeral Problem

Traditional luxury stabilizes value through materiality. Jean Baudrillard argued that luxury objects function as "durable signs of distinction" within social systems (Le Système des objets, 1968). They accumulate worth over time — through rarity, provenance, condition.

Haute gastronomy inverts this logic. A dish's existence is inseparable from its consumption. Once eaten, it leaves only sensory memory — unstable, private, impossible to verify.

Marcel Proust captured this intimacy in Du côté de chez Swann (1913). A madeleine dipped in tea triggers involuntary memory, resurfacing buried experiences with vivid intensity. But Proust's model depends on two conditions: embodied sensation (taste, smell) and unpredictability. You can't summon the memory on command. It emerges accidentally, years later, triggered by chance.

Contemporary culture rejects this uncertainty. We no longer wait for memories to surface — we demand they be captured, stored, and shared immediately.

"Luxury fixes value in the object, ensuring its role as a lasting marker of social differentiation."

— Jean Baudrillard, Le Système des objets
Temporal shift: Proust's involuntary memory vs. Instagram's instant archive

The Image Dictatorship

As digital memory systems prioritized the visible, gastronomic experience shifted its center of gravity. What once mattered as gustatory intensity now yields to visual power. Dishes are designed to be seen before they are tasted.

Food is no longer just consumed — it's performed for an audience. The plate becomes an image production platform, a visual trigger for projected desire.

Influencer Marketing Hub (2023 Benchmark Report):

  • 437,000 creators generated 1.2 million food/beverage posts
  • 75 billion impressions
  • 3 billion engagements

This dynamic reshapes product design. Many foods are now engineered not for flavor complexity, but for photographic impact.

Rainbow-colored ice cream, gold-leaf steaks, unicorn frappuccinos — chromatic intensity and ostentation override taste subtlety. Instagram virality becomes the primary design constraint.

"Popular Instagram food trends often prioritize aesthetic appeal over taste quality. Visual 'shareability' has become a core product requirement."

— Business Insider (2019)

Iconophagy — Consuming the Image

In a darkened room, Francis Dolarhyde stands before a table lit by soft, amber light. On it rests William Blake's watercolor: The Great Red Dragon and the Woman Clothed in Sun (1805). Slowly, almost ceremonially, he removes the fragile paper from its frame, brings it to his lips, and tears off a corner with his teeth. The taste of ink and paper fills his mouth. Bite by bite, the Dragon disappears — not destroyed, but absorbed.

This scene from Red Dragon (2002) crystallizes iconophagy: the act of consuming an image to internalize its power.

Contemporary foodporn mirrors this logic. We no longer consume only the dish — we consume its image. We absorb it visually, share it, accumulate it as social and emotional trace.

The iconophagic loop: photograph → share → consume the image

From Gastronomy to NFT — Preserving the Ephemeral

Contemporary luxury gastronomy increasingly integrates ephemeral aesthetics. Artist-decorated macarons, monumental chocolate sculptures, giant food installations — these creations are designed from the start to disappear.

We developed a speculative prototype to explore how edible artworks might be preserved and valorized after consumption.

Each macaron's surface is decorated with an original miniature artwork by a commissioned artist. Beneath the macaron, a printed QR code grants access to a certified digital version of the artwork, registered as an NFT on a public blockchain.

QR code integration — physical to digital translation

The gesture operates a controlled translation: the edible artwork, destined to vanish, migrates to a persistent digital existence.

This process opens critical perspectives for food design and haute gastronomy professions. The food photograph becomes not just an emotional archive, but an active relay for aesthetic capitalization.

Digital persistence: NFT interface mockup

Far from reducing gustatory experience to mere documentation, this device questions the possibility of an augmented memory of the ephemeral — a memory that extends, modulates, and redistributes traces in a space where food and image coexist in critical translation.

Reality Check — Limits and Ambiguities

Before celebrating this solution, we must examine its limitations.

Speculation vs. Accessibility: NFTs remain volatile speculative assets. The 2021-2022 crypto boom saw food-related NFTs trade for thousands of dollars. By 2023, most had lost 90%+ of their value.

Environmental Impact: Proof-of-Work blockchains consumed as much energy as entire countries. Even Proof-of-Stake systems carry non-negligible carbon footprints.

Technical Barriers: Not everyone owns a crypto wallet. Claiming an NFT requires MetaMask, seed phrases, gas fees, and blockchain literacy.

The Ownership Illusion: NFTs grant a blockchain record, but the actual image file is usually hosted off-chain. If the hosting service shuts down, your "permanent" NFT points to a dead link.

What happens when memory itself becomes encodable, transferable, purchasable? This project sits on a spectrum. Today: we encode the image of an experience. Tomorrow: we might encode the sensation itself.

The boundary between real and implanted memory collapses. If you can buy a memory of eating a Michelin-starred meal — complete with taste, smell, emotion — did you experience it or not?

— Philip K. Dick, We Can Remember It for You Wholesale (1966)

This is not science fiction. Neurotechnology advances toward memory modulation. DARPA funds research on memory encoding. Neuralink aims for brain-computer interfaces. The macaron NFT is a first step — a gentle rehearsal for a future where experiences themselves are capitalized, stored, traded, and implanted.

Toward a Memory Economy

Beyond attention economy lies memory economy — a system where memories become tradable goods, subject to market logic.

The macaron NFT already participates in this shift. By transforming an ephemeral experience into a persistent, authenticated trace, it rehearses a broader transformation.

The Chrysalis Metaphor

Throughout this project, a metaphor emerged: the gastronomic chrysalis.

A caterpillar consumes itself to become a butterfly. The macaron is consumed to release its digital twin. The physical artwork dissolves to birth its blockchain-certified persistence.

This metamorphosis is not neutral. It carries consequences:

  • The original is destroyed (you cannot keep both)
  • The digital trace is not identical to the physical experience
  • The transformation is irreversible

What Remains When Nothing Remains?

Faced with the programmed disappearance of gastronomic experience, contemporary culture has elaborated devices of visual capture and prolongation, engaging a progressive shift from lived experience to representation, from taste to image, from the ephemeral to the archive.

Through the speculative prototype presented here, we explored how a creation destined for consumption might migrate toward certified digital persistence, revealing new strategies for capitalizing on the perishable.

This dynamic sketches the contours of a broader transformation: the emergence of a memory economy, where experience itself becomes a product, susceptible to being captured, exchanged, and simulated.

Gastronomy — as the art of the ephemeral — reveals itself as a privileged terrain for understanding this mutation. No longer an art of immediate taste, but an art of programmable memory.

What remains of an experience when it is anticipated to be preserved, mediated to be exchanged, encoded to be possessed?

As our memories become capitalizable, perhaps the very capacity to live the moment dissolves.
